# Quality in Credentialing: How Trust Is Built

An introduction to quality assurance for credentials and to the VRITE criteria.

## About this course

**Course ID:** ICB-F09. **Framework alignment:** ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill QUAL "Quality assurance and accreditation" (VRITE pillar: Rigor), level L1 "Contribute", stage K "Knowledge".

**Skill at this level:** You explain why quality assurance matters for credential trust and you recognise common quality signals.

### Who this course is for

- Learning and development and corporate academy leads
- Programme and micro-credential leads
- Quality, registry and admissions staff
- Professional bodies and sector associations
- ICoBC Lead Reviewers and ICoBC Accredited Partners

No prior knowledge is needed. You do not need to run a credential programme to take this course, although a real or sample programme helps with the last module.

### Learning outcomes

1. Explain why digital credentials need quality assurance beyond technical verification.
2. Describe the five VRITE pillars and give an example question for each.
3. Distinguish internal quality assurance, external review, issuer accreditation and quality labels.
4. Place your own programme on a simple maturity scale.

### Workload

This is a self-paced course with about 2 hours of notional learning. Each module has short reading units, a worked example, a practice activity and a quiz. Facilitator notes describe optional live or workshop elements for teams who study together.

You complete a final knowledge check of 12 questions and you submit a self-rating sheet for an own or sample programme with short justifications. Both are described in the section "Assessment and badge".

## The trust problem

### Why a valid badge is not always a trusted badge

Imagine an HR coordinator at a corporate academy. She receives a digital badge from a job applicant. She clicks the verification link. The page confirms that the badge was issued by a named organisation to a named person on a given date, and that nobody has changed it. The technical check is complete and it passed.

She still has questions. What did the earner have to do to receive this badge? Who judged the evidence? Was the assessment hard or easy? Does the issuer have a record of awarding badges only when the criteria are met? The technical check answers none of these.

This is the trust problem. **Technical verification** shows that a credential is authentic, unaltered and issued by the stated issuer. It does not show that the learning behind the credential was worthwhile or that the assessment was sound. Those questions belong to **quality assurance**.

> A credential can be technically perfect and still tell the reader very little.

### What quality assurance means

Quality assurance (QA) is the set of planned activities that give confidence that a credential programme meets stated requirements. For a credential, this includes the design of the learning outcomes, the way learners are assessed, the way results are recorded, and the way the issuer handles complaints and errors.

QA covers the whole life of the credential:

- Before issue: the programme is designed with clear outcomes and criteria.
- At issue: assessment is carried out fairly and decisions are recorded.
- After issue: the issuer keeps records, handles appeals and reviews the programme.

### Two layers of trust

It helps to think of two layers.

| Layer | Question it answers | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Technical trust | Is this credential real, unaltered and from the stated issuer? | Digital signatures, verification links, open data standards |
| Quality trust | Does this credential stand for something meaningful and well assessed? | Published criteria, assessment records, review, accreditation, quality labels |

Both layers are needed. Without technical trust, a reader cannot rely on the data. Without quality trust, a reader cannot rely on the meaning.

### Who is affected when quality is weak

Weak quality harms several groups at the same time:

- **Earners** hold credentials that employers and admissions teams may discount.
- **Employers and admissions staff** spend time checking, or make poor decisions.
- **Providers with strong programmes** are harder to tell apart from providers with weak ones.
- **Professional bodies and policy makers** find it harder to support credentials in regulation or recruitment.

The EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (2022) reflects this concern. It encourages quality assurance of micro-credentials and transparency of their key elements. Read the text itself for the exact wording, since this course gives only a summary.

**Worked example: Two applicants, two badges**

*Context A: a university.* An admissions officer at a university receives two applications for a postgraduate course. Each applicant lists a data analysis micro-credential. Both badges verify correctly. The first badge links to a page that shows learning outcomes, a description of a graded project and the name of the assessor's unit. The second badge links to a page that shows only a title and an image.

The officer treats the first badge as useful evidence and the second as a lead to follow up. Both are technically valid. Only the first helps the reader judge quality.

*Context B: a corporate academy.* A talent manager at a large employer reviews internal badges awarded by the company's academy. One team awards a "Project leadership" badge after attendance at a workshop. Another team awards the same badge after a workplace task reviewed by two senior colleagues. The talent manager cannot tell the two apart from the badge title. A shared quality standard inside the academy would remove this problem.

All details in these examples are illustrative.

**Practice activity: Spot the missing information**

Take a digital credential you have received, issued or seen. It can be a badge, a certificate or a sample from your organisation. Open the verification page if one exists. Write down:

1. What the technical check tells you.
2. Three questions about quality that the page does not answer.
3. Who could answer those questions.

Keep your notes. You will use them in Module 2.

[( )] A shortage of badge images
[(X)] A gap in quality assurance and transparency

## Quality signals readers look for

### What a signal is

A **quality signal** is a piece of information that helps a reader judge how far to trust a credential. Readers include employers, admissions officers, registrars, professional bodies and earners themselves. No single signal proves quality. Several signals together build a picture.

Signals come from three places: the credential itself, the issuer, and independent parties.

### Signals inside the credential

A well-formed credential tells the reader what it stands for. Look for:

- **Clear learning outcomes:** what the earner can explain or do.
- **Assessment description:** how achievement was judged, for example a project, an exam or observed practice.
- **Criteria for award:** what had to be met, stated in plain words.
- **Level and size:** where it sits in a framework and how much learning it represents, where a framework is used.
- **Dates and validity:** when it was earned and whether it expires.
- **Evidence link:** a way to see examples of the work or the assessment outcome, with the earner's consent.

The EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (2022) lists elements that a micro-credential should describe, such as learning outcomes, workload and the type of assessment. Check the official text for the full list.

### Signals about the issuer

The issuer's own practice also matters. Readers look for:

- A published description of how the programme is designed and reviewed.
- Named roles responsible for assessment and quality.
- A policy for appeals, corrections and withdrawal of credentials.
- A record of consistent decisions over time.
- Openness about what the credential does not claim.

### Signals from independent parties

Independent signals come from outside the issuer:

- **External review** of the programme by experts who are not part of the delivery team.
- **Accreditation** of the issuer or programme by a recognised body.
- **Quality labels** or seals that show a programme met a stated set of criteria.
- **Recognition** by a professional body, sector association or public authority.

Each of these signals has a scope. A quality label may cover one programme. Accreditation may cover an institution. Readers should check what exactly was reviewed and when.

### Warning signs

Some patterns suggest that more checking is needed:

- No description of assessment, or a vague one such as "participation".
- Very large claims for very little learning time.
- No named issuer contact and no complaints route.
- A seal or logo with no explanation of who awards it or against which criteria.
- Credentials that can be bought without any assessment.

A warning sign is a prompt to ask questions. It is not proof of poor quality.

### Reading signals together

A reader can use a simple routine: look inside the credential, look at the issuer, then look for independent confirmation. If two of the three are strong and the third is silent, the reader can ask for more. If all three are silent, the reader should treat the credential as an unverified claim about learning.

**Worked example: Reading three signals in two settings**

*Context A: a training provider.* A registrar at a college receives a transfer request. The student holds a micro-credential from another provider. The registrar checks the credential page. It lists four learning outcomes, describes a practical assessment marked by two assessors, and states a size of 60 hours of learning (illustrative). The issuer's website explains its appeals route and names a quality officer. A professional body lists the programme as recognised. The registrar finds strong signals in all three places and grants credit towards a related unit.

*Context B: a corporate academy.* A learning lead at a corporate academy reviews a vendor-neutral external badge that a new hire lists. The badge page names the issuer and the date but gives no outcomes and no assessment description. The issuer's website has no appeals policy. The badge shows a round logo that says "Quality approved" with no explanation. The learning lead records the credential as an unverified claim and asks the new hire to describe what they did to earn it.

## VRITE: Value, Rigor, Impact, Trustworthiness, Experience

### A note on status

The ICoBC VRITE criteria are **Emerging**. Indicators have not yet been published. Internal documents use different expansions of the acronym, and pilots are ongoing. This module uses the five names in the course title as a working structure and offers example questions for each pillar. The questions are teaching aids. They are not official indicators. The Global Quality Seal is also Emerging, so this course does not describe its requirements.

Status as of November 2026; check before publishing.

### The five pillars

VRITE gives five lenses for asking whether a credential deserves trust. Each pillar is a group of questions.

**Value** asks whether the credential matters to the people who use it. A credential has value when earners, employers, education providers or professional bodies see a real purpose in it.

Example question: *Which users asked for this credential, and what decisions will it help them make?*

**Rigor** asks whether the design and assessment are sound. Rigor covers clear outcomes, fit between outcomes and assessment, qualified assessors and consistent decisions.

Example question: *How do we know that the assessment measures the stated learning outcomes?*

**Impact** asks what difference the credential makes. This includes outcomes for earners, for employers and for the issuer's own aims. Impact needs evidence over time, not only at launch.

Example question: *What evidence do we collect on what earners do with the credential after award?*

**Trustworthiness** asks whether the issuer and the data can be relied upon. This pillar covers identity, security, privacy, honest claims, handling of errors and openness about limits.

Example question: *What happens if we award a credential in error, and how is the earner and the reader informed?*

**Experience** asks how the credential feels and works for people. It covers clarity of information, accessibility, fairness, support for earners and ease of use for readers.

Example question: *Can an earner with access needs complete the assessment on equal terms?*

### How the pillars relate to each other

The pillars overlap and support each other. A programme with high rigor but no value serves nobody. A programme with strong value but weak trustworthiness may lose users after a single error. Reviewers look at the balance as well as each pillar.

The ICSF skill QUAL, "Quality assurance and accreditation", is placed under the Rigor pillar. This reflects that quality assurance is the main route by which rigor is built and shown. Quality assurance still touches all five pillars.

### Using VRITE as a question set

At this stage, VRITE works best as a set of prompts for discussion and planning. A programme team can take one pillar at a time and ask: what do we already do, what do we say publicly, and what evidence do we hold? Gaps become an action list.

When indicators are published, teams will be able to match their evidence to them. Until then, keep your evidence in a form that is easy to share: dated, named, and linked to the pillar it supports.

### AI-assisted assessment: questions to add

Some programmes use AI tools to help with assessment, for example to give feedback on drafts, to score short answers or to flag possible plagiarism. This raises quality questions under several pillars.

- **Rigor:** Has the tool been checked against human judgement on a sample of work? Who decides the final result?
- **Trustworthiness:** What data is shared with the tool, and where is it processed? How is the earner told that AI is used?
- **Experience:** Can an earner ask for human review of a result? Are there fair routes for those whom the tool serves poorly?
- **Value and Impact:** Does AI assistance improve the credential's usefulness, and how do you know?

Keep a record of the answers. A reader who learns that AI was used will reasonably ask the same questions.

## Internal and external quality assurance

### Four terms that are often mixed up

People use the words "quality", "accreditation", "label" and "review" loosely. This module separates four ideas so that you can use them precisely.

### Internal quality assurance

**Internal quality assurance** is carried out by the issuer itself. The issuer sets standards, checks its own work and acts on what it finds. Typical activities are:

- Written criteria for each credential.
- Training and calibration of assessors, so that different assessors reach similar decisions.
- Moderation, where a second person checks a sample of decisions.
- Collection and review of feedback from earners and readers.
- Regular review of the programme, with recorded changes.

Internal QA is the foundation. Without it, external parties have little to review.

### External review

**External review** is a check by people who are independent of the delivery team. Reviewers may be experts from another institution, an employer panel, a subject association or an external examiner. They look at the design, the assessment and the evidence, and they give written feedback. External review supports a programme. It does not necessarily confer a formal status.

### Issuer accreditation

**Issuer accreditation** is a formal decision by a recognised body that an issuer meets stated standards. The decision usually covers the organisation's processes and capacity: governance, assessment systems, record keeping, complaints handling and review. Accreditation is time-limited and is normally followed by monitoring.

Accreditation applies to the issuer or to defined programmes. Always check its scope: which body granted it, what it covers, and from when to when.

### Quality labels

A **quality label** or **seal** is a mark given to a programme or credential that meets a defined set of criteria. A label is usually specific to a programme and may be easier to obtain than accreditation. Labels differ widely. Some come from public bodies, some from associations, and some from private schemes.

The ICoBC Global Quality Seal is **Emerging**. Its indicators are not yet published and pilots are ongoing, so this course does not describe its requirements.

Status as of November 2026; check before publishing.

### A comparison

| Feature | Internal QA | External review | Issuer accreditation | Quality label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who carries it out | The issuer | Independent reviewers | A recognised accrediting body | A label owner |
| Main focus | Day-to-day practice and improvement | Independent judgement on design and evidence | Whether the issuer meets standards | Whether a programme meets label criteria |
| Typical scope | Programme or whole issuer | Programme | Issuer or defined programmes | Programme or credential |
| Result | Records, improvements | Report and recommendations | Formal status for a period | A mark |

### How they fit together

The four elements build on each other. Internal QA produces the evidence. External review tests it. Accreditation recognises that the issuer's systems are sound. A label tells readers that a particular credential meets stated criteria. A reader benefits most when the scope of each element is stated clearly and each one can be checked.

The EU Council Recommendation on micro-credentials (2022) points to internal and external quality assurance and to the role of existing quality frameworks. Check the official text for the exact wording and for national transpositions.

## Where are you now?

### A simple maturity scale

A maturity scale helps a team see where it stands and what to do next. The scale below has five steps. It is a teaching tool for this course. It is not an official ICoBC rating, and it does not predict the result of any review or label.

**Step 1: Ad hoc.** Credentials are issued without written criteria. Decisions depend on individuals. There are few or no records.

**Step 2: Defined.** Criteria and learning outcomes are written for each credential. Assessment methods are described. Records of decisions exist, though they may be incomplete.

**Step 3: Managed.** Roles are named. Assessors are briefed. A sample of decisions is checked by a second person. Complaints and corrections follow a written process.

**Step 4: Reviewed.** The programme is reviewed on a regular schedule using feedback and evidence. External reviewers or advisers give independent comments. Changes are recorded.

**Step 5: Recognised.** The issuer or programme holds accreditation, a quality label or similar recognition, with a stated scope, and keeps improving. Evidence is public and easy to check.

### How to use the scale

Rate the programme as a whole, but also rate each VRITE pillar if you can. A programme may be at Step 4 for Rigor and at Step 2 for Experience. The unevenness shows where to act.

Be honest and specific. A rating should rest on evidence you can name: a document, a record, a date, a person. If you cannot point to evidence, rate one step lower.

A higher step is not always the right goal. A small internal badge may be well served by Step 3. A badge used for hiring or regulation across organisations may need Steps 4 and 5. Match the step to the stakes.

### Writing a short justification

For each rating, write two or three sentences:

1. The step you chose.
2. One piece of evidence that supports it.
3. One action that would move you to the next step.

Short justifications are part of the badge evidence for this course.

### Next steps after this course

This course teaches knowledge and recognition. It does not make you an assessor or a reviewer. If you want to act on your self-rating, plan small steps: write down missing criteria, name responsible roles, set a review date and invite one external reader. Further ICoBC courses address practice at higher stages and levels.

### Rubric for the self-rating sheet

The self-rating sheet is checked against the rubric below. It is reviewed for clarity and honesty, not for a high score.

| Criterion | Not yet | Meets | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Programme described | No description of the programme | Programme named and purpose stated in one or two sentences | Programme, audience and stakes are clear |
| Ratings given | One or more pillars missing | All five pillars rated on the five-step scale | All five pillars and an overall step given, with notes on uneven results |
| Evidence named | No evidence named | Evidence named for most pillars | Evidence named for every pillar and dated or located |
| Justifications | Opinions only | Short reasons that refer to evidence | Short reasons that refer to evidence and say why a lower or higher step was rejected |
| Next actions | No actions | One action per pillar | One realistic action per pillar, with a role or date |
| Use of VRITE questions | No questions used | One question per pillar used | Questions used and one AI-assessment question added where relevant |

### Pass rule

- **Knowledge check:** at least 9 of 12 questions correct (75 per cent). You may repeat the check.
- **Self-rating sheet:** at least "Meets" in every criterion of the rubric.
- Both parts are needed for the badge.

### Badge evidence

You submit:

1. Confirmation that you passed the knowledge check.
2. Your completed self-rating sheet.

The badge records that you completed this Knowledge course at Level 1 and met the pass rule. It does not prove competence in a job role.
